A B S T R A C T
One of the challenges in rice field management is that traditional drone-based pesticide spraying relies on 
uniform dosing, which cannot be dynamically adjusted based on the types and distribution of the weeds. This can 
lead to resource waste and environmental pollution. To achieve precise spraying at variable rates, weed iden­
tification to understand the current distribution of weeds is a critical task. In this context, for weed segmentation 
in rice field remote sensing images, we propose a novel segmentation model called CTFFNet that combines 
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Transformer. We design two feature extraction modules using 
Transformer and Residual CNN, which generate global semantic features and local visual features. To effectively 
fuse these complementary features, we use attention mechanisms in the feature fusion stage for dynamic feature 
selection and integration. This helps the model automatically learn the importance of different features for 
adaptive integration of global and local information. Additionally, in the decoding stage, we apply Deformable 
Convolutional Networks (DCN) for upsampling to capture variations in weed object shapes, resulting in more 
sensitive segmentation predictions. Experiments on our self-built rice field weed dataset demonstrate that, 
compared to single CNN or Transformer models, our fusion framework achieves the best segmentation accuracy 
when dealing with complex and variable weed object shapes, with an MIoU of 72.8, significantly improving 
segmentation performance.
1. Introduction
High-quality and high-yield rice production is of paramount impor­
tance to ensure national food security, and weeds are one of the sig­
nificant factors affecting rice growth (Abdullah et al., 2023). Barnyard 
grass and Sagittaria trifolia are among the most common and harmful 
weeds in rice fields. Barnyard grass belongs to the same botanical family 
as rice, displaying a remarkable visual similarity and having a similar 
growth period. It has the ability to produce a substantial number of 
seeds, each plant producing more than a thousand seeds that spread 
throughout the fields, leading to more severe barnyard grass contami­
nation in the following year (Zhang et al., 2017). Sagittaria trifolia, 
characterized by its tall and robust stems and leaves, significantly shade 
rice plants, competes for water, nutrients,and space, and has a well- 
developed root system that absorbs soil nutrients extensively, 
depleting soil fertility. Furthermore, the decaying leaves of Sagittaria 
trifolia can produce toxic substances, leading to soil pollution (Ding, 
2020). According to statistics, losses due to weeds in rice production in 
China exceed 15 %, with severe cases exceeding 20 %. Therefore, 
effective control measures must be implemented for barnyard grass and 
Sagittaria trifolia during rice production management.
In recent years, with the improvement in payload capacity and 
performance of agricultural drones, aerial pesticide application has 
become the main method for weed control in rice fields (Rosle et al., 
2021). Since weeds in rice fields are not uniformly distributed, the use of 
agricultural drones for large-scale herbicide spraying is bound to leave a 
significant amount of chemical residues on rice surfaces and soil. This 
not only leads to pesticide wastage and environmental pollution, but 
also increases the risk of crops developing herbicide resistance, resulting 
in additional economic losses (Huang, 2019). Precise identification of 
weeds and variable rate pesticide application based on weed density are 
effective approaches to address this problem.

With the continuous development and improvement of UAV remote 
sensing technology, UAV remote sensing can obtain high-resolution 
remote sensing images of weeds in rice fields. Compared to manual 
ground-based weed image collection, UAV remote sensing offers ad­
vantages such as greater flexibility, faster acquisition, high spatial res­
olution, and the ability to generate prescription maps for pesticide 
application. As the only means currently capable of continuous, effi­
cient, and repeatable earth observation, UAV remote sensing has 
demonstrated tremendous potential in the field of weed recognition 
(Bansod et al., 2017).
Machine vision-based identification methods have been widely 
adopted in weed recognition research. Based on the different visual 
characteristics exhibited by weeds in images, manually extracted fea­
tures, such as color, texture, and morphology,are used to build feature 
vectors for weed image classification through machine learning models, 
achieving the goal of weed identification (Wang et al., 2019; Bakh­
shipour and Jafari, 2018).Zhang et al. (2015) applied preprocessing 
techniques, including transformation and segmentation, to extract 13 
color and invariant moment features from corn field weed images. They 
used a feature selection method to choose the most effective features and 
constructed feature vectors. An Improved Probabilistic Neural Network 
(IPNN) was used to identify corn weeds, achieving recognition rates of 
more than 95 %. Kazmi et al. (2015) introduced a novel local vegetation 
color descriptor and integrated vegetation indices into local features. 
They assessed the precision of weed detection in sugar beet fields using 
KNN and SVM classifiers, with accuracy exceeding 80 %.Machine 
learning approaches require manual feature extraction, and feature 
patterns are relatively fixed, leading to limited generalization and 
adaptability to different environments. Deep learning, with its advan­
tages of automatic feature learning, hierarchical feature representation, 
and end-to-end training, is increasingly applied in weed recognition 
research. Teimouri et al. (2018) used Convolutional Neural Networks 
(CNN) to automatically identify 18 weed species, providing new 
research avenues for weed recognition in smart weeding tasks. The 
method achieved the highest accuracy for the Rumex genus, with an 
accuracy of 78 %, while the accuracy was the lowest for blackgrass, at 
46 %. Peng et al. (2020) utilized various structures of the VGG16 deep 
convolutional neural network to automatically extract characteristics 
from six types of rice field weeds. Their results showed that the VGG16- 
SGD deep learning model achieved the highest accuracy, with recogni­
tion rates of over 90 % for all six rice field weeds. Huang (2019)
collected data from 10 m above ground using drones for four types of 
rice field weeds. They used a Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) for the 
pixel-level classification of rice field remote sensing images, achieving 
an accuracy of 80.2 % on the test set.
Crops have different row and ridge spacing, with clear patterns in 
their arrangement, while weeds grow haphazardly, resulting in posi­
tional differences between the two. Some researchers have begun to 
incorporate contextual information that represents positional differ­
ences between crops and weeds as one of the features in weed recog­
nition studies. Transformer models, known for their robust contextual 
modeling capabilities, have shown remarkable performance in this 
context. Liu et al. (2022) used the Vision Transformer model to recog­
nize weeds from 38 different varieties in tea gardens, achieving a 
maximum classification accuracy of 97.31 %. Jiang et al. (2022) eval­
uated the weed segmentation results for three types of Transformer ar­
chitectures, including Swin Transformer, SegFormer, and Segmenter, in 
a dataset containing 10 weed categories. Among these, SegFormer 
achieved an average Intersection over Union (IoU) accuracy of 65.74 %.
In summarizing the current state of research both domestically and 
internationally, it’s evident that Transformer models and CNN models 
have their respective strengths and weaknesses when applied to 
different tasks and datasets. CNN models heavily rely on convolutional 
layers, which extract features within small local regions of input data, 
achieving localized perception. These models are effective in capturing 
local characteristics but may have limitations in capturing global 
information (Song et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022a; Liu 
et al., 2022b). Their limited receptive field means they are more focused 
on local feature extraction, which can lead to weaker performance in 
capturing global information. On the other hand, transformer models, 
with the incorporation of self-attention mechanisms and positional 
encodings, excel at understanding dependencies between different po­
sitions in an image. They are particularly skilled at capturing long- 
distance relationships between elements in the data (Yuan et al., 
2023a; Yuan et al., 2023b; Huang et al., 2022). When dealing with 
scenarios in which barnyard grass and rice exhibit extremely similar 
appearances, especially in remote sensing images in which texture and 
color features have minimal differences, CNN models may struggle with 
effective segmentation. In contrast, Transformer models shine in 
capturing contextual information, making them well-suited for handling 
such complex situations. For Sagittaria trifolia, which is smaller in size 
in remote sensing images but has a significantly different appearance 
from rice, CNN models are more appropriate for semantic segmentation 
due to their effectiveness in capturing local features and shapes.
In order to achieve effective semantic segmentation of both barnyard 
grass and Sagittaria trifolia, this study introduces a network called 
CTFFNet that combines the strengths of both CNN and Transformer 
models, addressing their individual weaknesses. We begin by designing 
a CNN-based feature extraction module using MobileNetV2 to primarily 
extract local feature information related to rice field weeds. Simulta­
neously, a Transformer-based feature extraction module is designed 
using Transformer layers to capture long-range dependencies. Subse­
quently, we employ a Convolutional Block Attention Module(CBAM) to 
create a feature fusion module, which enhances the model’s focus on 
critical areas and channels of the fused features. Additionally, we utilize 
deformable convolutions at the decoding stage to further enhance the 
model’s segmentation performance. The main contributions of this 
research are summarized as follows: 
• To address the challenge of recognizing multiple weed species in rice 
field remote sensing images, we propose a semantic segmentation 
model that combines Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and 
Transformer features. The objective is to leverage CNN for local 
texture feature extraction and Transformer for learning global de­
pendencies, enabling accurate identification of different weed spe­
cies in complex environments.
• We propose the use of attention mechanisms to construct a feature 
fusion module, which allows the automatic learning of the impor­
tance of different features, adjusts the dependencies between feature 
layers, and dynamically integrates and utilizes global and local in­
formation. This enhancement improves the model’s ability to 
represent semantic features effectively.
• Given the significant variation in the shapes of weed targets in rice 
field remote sensing image segmentation tasks, we apply deformable 
convolutions in the decoding stage. This approach allows dynamic 
learning to generate deformable convolution kernels that match the 
shapes of the targets, preserving fine boundary details in the seg­
mentation results.
2. Experimental design
2.1. Data collection
Due to variations in weed control measures and herbicide selection 
in the rice production process, different dominant weeds can be found in 
rice fields. In the rice production process, farmers usually adopt a “two 
seals and one kill” weed control strategy, which involves applying soil 
sealing herbicides before rice transplanting to preliminarily control 
weeds in the field; spraying foliar herbicides again during the green 
period of rice to further remove weeds in the field; If there are still weed 
residues during the tillering stage of rice, farmers will apply targeted 
stem and leaf spraying to areas with weeds in order to completely

targeted weeding. The experiment took place in the training field of

2.2. Dataset Generation
During the drone’s image collection, adjacent images have an 80 % 
overlap region. If we directly annotate the original images, human errors 
may cause inconsistencies or omissions in the labeling of the same target 
in different images, which will affect the training accuracy of the deep 
learning model. To avoid this, our research first used DJI Terra(V3.4.4, 
DJI Innovation Company, Shenzhen, China) to align and fuse the 2022 
rice weed remote sensing data, and then cut the aligned and fused im­
ages into 600x600 pixel nonoverlapping subimages. In the field, weed 
distribution exhibits non-uniform patterns, with dense areas showing 
continuous weed growth, while sparse areas have individual weed 
plants. Therefore, during the manual labeling process, the barnyard 
grass was classified into two groups: continuous barnyard grass and 
individual barnyard grass. Sagittaria trifolia is a perennial plant that 
tends to grow more scatteredly between individuals rather than forming 
continuous growth, so it was not classified. Manual labeling was per­
formed using the Labelme software (v4.5.6), employing a polygonal 
annotation method. The points were manually densely selected along 
the contour of the weed objects in the image,and connected to create 
closed polygon regions that closely matched the boundaries. All pixels 
within these regions were labeled as weed categories. The dataset is 
illustrated in Fig. 2, and the number of samples after labeling is provided 
in Table 2. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets in a 
7:3 ratio, with no overlapp data between sets.
2.3. Data Augmentation
To enhance the performance, robustness, and generalization of deep 
learning models, this stud used various data augmentation techniques 
on the training dataset, including random cropping, color jitter, noise 
addition, and random rotation. This expansion increased the size of the 
training dataset to ten times its original volume.
3. Materials and methods
3.1. Methods used in this study
The CTFFNet model is a pixel-level segmentation model designed for 
barnyard grass and Sagittaria trifolia, classifying each pixel into four 
categories: bund, connected barnyard grass, single barnyard grass and 
Sagittaria trifolia. The model framework is illustrated in Fig. 3. The 
network consists primarily of three components: a CNN-based feature 
extraction module, a Transformer-based feature extraction module, and 
a feature fusion module. The motivation for using both the CNN feature 
extraction module and the Transformer feature extraction module is that 
each has its advantages in semantic segmentation of barnyard grass and 
Sagittaria trifolia, and they complement each other. The convolution 
and pooling operations in CNN are effective in extracting local infor­
mation such as textures and shapes from images. This module focuses 
primarily on capturing local features and details, especially for smaller 
targets that have significant differences from rice, such as Sagittaria 
trifolia (Baker et al., 2020; Islam et al., 2021). On the other hand, the 
Transformer excels at capturing global contextual information. Through 
self-attention mechanisms, it can capture long-distance dependencies 
between objects, establishing context information between targets and 
the entire remote sensing image. This helps eliminate confusion caused 
by the similarity between barnyard grass and rice (Ding and Jia, 2021; 
Khan et al., 2021). The feature fusion module is employed to merge the 
features extracted by the CNN and Transformer encoders. By leveraging 
the strengths of both, it aims to better address the challenges of target 
similarity and the small size of the targets in remote sensing images,

CNN Feature Extraction Module: In order to extract local features of 
rice and weeds while reducing the computational load of the model, this 
study uses the MobileNetV2 network (Sandler et al., 2018) as CNN 
feature extractor. The MobileNetV2 network consists of convolution 
layers, grouping layers, and a series of bottleneck structures. The 
structure is depicted in Fig. 4. MobileNetV2 achieves an approximately 9 
times reduction in computation compared to traditional convolutions by 
separating the conventional 3 × 3 convolution into 3 × 3 depthwise 
convolution (DW) and 1 × 1 pointwise convolution (PW) (Ye and Zhang, 
2022). In the CNN feature extraction module, for each image I, local 
features of rice and weeds are extracted by applying convolution and 
pooling operations from the MobileNetV2 network. The local features 
extracted are denoted as F2. To manage computational costs, the 
resolution of the feature maps is reduced to 16 × 16 with 320 channels.
Transformer Feature Extraction Module: Utilizing contextual infor­
mation provides the model with crucial prior knowledge, enabling it to 
go beyond predicting solely based on local features and allowing for a 
more fine-grained image understanding and segmentation from a global 
perspective. CNNs heavily rely on convolution operations with strong 
inductive biases, inevitably leading to a non-negligible challenge of 
limited receptive fields. Various solutions have been proposed to address 
this issue, such as Atrous convolutions, increasing kernel sizes, and 
pyramid pooling, among others. Regardless of the method used, the 
standard CNN model architecture remains unchanged (Yuan et al., 
2023). In recent years, due to their powerful representation capability 
for long-range dependencies, Transformer-based models have achieved 
remarkable accuracy in various NLP tasks. Some researchers have pro­
posed Transformer-based image segmentation models to tackle the 
limited receptive field problem of CNNs and have achieved results 
comparable to CNN-based methods (Carion et al., 2020).

transforming the three-dimensional data into one-dimensional vectors. 
Consequently, the input sequence has a length of 196. The dimension of

3.2. Model training and evaluation metrics
Training Details: Training the model is performed end-to-end, where 
the input is the original image, and the output is the corresponding 
segmentation map without manual intervention. Transfer learning is 
employed during model training. The layers within the Transformer 
feature extraction module are initialized with pre-trained weights from 
the ImageNet-1 K dataset (Deng et al., 2009) (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020). 
Pre-trained MobileNetV2 weights are used to initialize the parameters of 
our CNN encoder. Subsequently, the weights of the feature fusion 
component are fine-tuned on our dataset, enabling the model to 
converge more quickly. The ImageNet dataset consists of approximately 
1.2 million images covering 1,000 different object and scene categories 
(Huh et al., 2016; Russakovsky et al., 2014). In this research task, its rich 
variety of categories (including plant categories) and the large number 
of images allow the Transformer feature extraction network to be pre- 
trained to a relatively optimal weight space, significantly enhancing 
the feature extraction’s generalization. Building on this, the model’s 
weights are fine-tuned based on the specific categories present in the rice 
and weed remote sensing dataset, making it easier to reach a global 
optimum. Taking into account physical memory and learning efficiency, 
each batch is set to contain 16 training images and the maximum 
number of iterations is set to 1000. The model employs the Stochastic 
Gradient Descent optimizer (SGD). The learning rate (lr) decay strategy

6) Comparison on public datasets
To more comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the 
improvement methods proposed in this study, as well as the perfor­
mance and generalization capability of CTFFNet, we conducted a com­
parison of CTFFNet, its improved variants, and classical semantic 
segmentation models on the Pascal VOC2007 public dataset. Pascal 
VOC2007 is a widely used and significant dataset in the field of com­
puter vision, containing 20 common object categories such as potted 
plants, flowers, birds, etc., with a total of approximately 9,963 images 
and 24,640 labeled objects. It includes objects of varying sizes, poses, 
and occlusions. Due to its diversity and complexity, this dataset has 
become one of the benchmark datasets in the fields of image processing 
and object detection. Several classical semantic segmentation algo­
rithms were selected as comparison models in this study, including FCN- 
ResNet50, Deeplabv3+, U-Net, and SETR. To measure the performance 
of the models, we adopted the most commonly used evaluation metric in 
semantic segmentation tasks—MIoU. Since machine learning experi­
ments are often subject to random factors, a single experiment may 
produce fluctuating results. To ensure the reliability and stability of the 
experimental results, three independent experiments were conducted 
for each model, and the average of the three results was taken as the final 
evaluation. The experimental results are shown in Table 7.
As shown in the table, CTFFNet achieved an MIoU of 74.3 %, 
delivering the best overall segmentation performance among all the 
compared models. On the Pascal VOC2007 dataset, CTFFNet achieved 
the highest segmentation accuracy in four categories: Boat, Cow, Pot­
tedplant, and Sheep, fully demonstrating the effectiveness of the im­
provements made to the model in this study.
A detailed analysis shows that models using only CNN architectures, 
such as FCN-ResNet, Deeplabv3+, and U-Net, achieved the best seg­
mentation results in five categories. Models using only Transformer ar­
chitectures, such as SETR, “TFE + Conv”, and “TFE + DCN”, performed 
well in seven categories. However, the hybrid model CTFFNet, which 
combines CNN and Transformer architectures, performed exceptionally 
well in 13 categories, outperforming the other models. This indicates 
that the fusion of CNN and Transformer architectures significantly 
improved the overall segmentation performance and robustness of the 
model. In the PASCAL VOC 2007 dataset, Pottedplant is classified as a 
small object category. CTFFNet achieved a segmentation accuracy of 
61.6 % (MIoU) for this category, significantly outperforming the other 
models and showing an improvement of 1.9 % over the second-best 
model. This further illustrates that the combination of CNN and Trans­
former architectures greatly enhances the model’s ability to recognize 
and segment small objects.
In previous studies, most researchers applied deformable convolu­
tions to the encoder side to enhance the receptive field and contextual 
modeling capabilities by dynamically adjusting the shape and size of the 
convolutional kernels. In this study, however, we attempt to apply 
deformable convolutions to the decoder side to better address the issue 
of feature misalignment, thereby achieving more accurate semantic in­
formation when restoring image details. Experimental results show that 
the “TFE + DCN” model, which incorporates deformable convolutions, 
achieved an MIoU of 73.2 % on the PASCAL VOC 2007 dataset, repre­
senting a 3 % improvement compared to the original “TFE + Conv” 
model. This validates that applying deformable convolutions on the 
decoder side can also effectively enhance the performance of the 
CTFFNet model in image semantic segmentation tasks.
The CTFFNet network, which integrates the CBAM module for 
feature fusion, achieved a significant improvement in image segmenta­
tion accuracy compared to the “Concat + DCN” model that uses only 
simple convolutions for feature fusion. Specifically, the MIoU of the 
CTFFNet model is 1 % higher than that of the “Concat + DCN” model. 
This improvement is mainly attributed to the channel attention mech­
anism in the CBAM module, which assigns different weights to each 
channel of the input feature map, helping the model to more effectively 
capture key features in the image. Additionally, the spatial attention 
mechanism in the CBAM module guides the model to focus on regions of 
interest, further improving the accuracy of segmentation details. How­
ever, experimental results also show that over-stacking the CBAM 
modules may lead to a decrease in model accuracy. For example, in the 
“CTFFNet + CBAM” network, introducing the CBAM module into both 
the CNN feature extraction module and the feature fusion module 
resulted in an MIoU decrease of 0.8 % compared to the CTFFNet 
network. This indicates that when using attention mechanisms, careful 
consideration should be given to balancing the number of modules with 
model performance to avoid the loss of useful information due to 
excessive attention.

Additionally, one of the major weeds in rice fields—Barnyard Grass­

morphological structure and growth habits. This similarity, combined 
with the small target size of Barnyard Grass and rice in drone remote 
sensing images and the complex, ever-changing background environ­

in rice fields based on drone remote sensing imagery.
To address the aforementioned issues, this study first used drones to 
collect high-resolution remote sensing images of Barnyard Grass and 
Sagittaria trifolia in rice fields, creating a dataset specifically for weed 
identification in rice fields. In order to effectively distinguish Barnyard 
Grass, which is highly similar to rice, this study proposes a weed iden­
tification method that combines CNN and Transformers. Specifically, 
traditional CNNs are used initially to extract local texture features, 
capturing subtle morphological differences between Barnyard Grass and 
rice. However, relying solely on local texture features is insufficient to 
differentiate between such highly similar species. Since rice is arranged 
in neat rows with fixed spacing between rows and field ridges, while 
Barnyard Grass grows chaotically, there is a significant difference in 
their spatial distribution. Therefore, this study further incorporates a 
Transformer feature extraction module to capture the contextual infor­
mation related to the position and distribution of crops and weeds. This 
improvement enhanced the model’s accuracy by 1.8 %. The reason lies 
in the Transformer’s self-attention and multi-head attention mecha­
nisms, which allow it to focus on all positions in the input sequence 
simultaneously, capturing long-range dependencies and global contex­
tual information. Combined with positional encoding, the Transformer 
can understand both the relative and absolute positions of elements, 
further enhancing its ability to model context.
When processing weed images, CNN and Transformer, due to their 
different network structures and learning mechanisms, respectively 
focus on local detailed features and global features in remote sensing 
images of weeds in rice fields. This provides complementary information 
to the model. To fully utilize these different types of features and avoid 
information loss, this study introduces the CBAM to perform feature 
fusion. CBAM, through its channel attention and spatial attention 
modules, effectively guides the network to focus on the most discrimi­
native feature areas. Channel attention selects which channels contain 
more useful information, while spatial attention helps the model iden­
tify key regions in the remote sensing images of rice fields. In this way, 
the CTFFNet model can capture multi-dimensional information, allow­
ing for a more comprehensive understanding of weed images, ultimately 
improving the model’s weed recognition capability. Experimental re­
sults show that the introduction of the CBAM module increased the weed 
recognition accuracy by 0.2 %, confirming the effectiveness of feature 
fusion. To further explore the optimal placement of the CBAM module, 
we conducted comparative experiments by inserting the CBAM module 
at different positions in the model and observing its impact on perfor­
mance. The results indicate that although the CBAM module can effec­
tively fuse features, excessive stacking of attention modules leads to 
over-filtering of the information flow. The main function of attention 
mechanisms is to select important features and suppress irrelevant ones, 
but excessive attention stacking may cause useful features to be 
mistakenly filtered out after multiple layers of selection. This over- 
filtering may reduce the model’s sensitivity to certain details and 
diverse features, thereby limiting its learning ability.
Barnyard Grass and Sagittaria trifolia, as common agricultural 
weeds, exhibit highly variable morphologies and irregular leaf shapes, 
posing challenges for precise identification. Traditional convolution 
operations are limited by fixed receptive fields in size and shape, making 
it difficult to capture the irregular deformations of targets like Barnyard 
Grass, which negatively affects recognition accuracy. To address this 
issue, we introduced deformable convolution in the decoding stage. 
Deformable convolution incorporates learnable offset parameters, 
allowing the convolutional kernels to dynamically adapt to the shape of 
the target. This method enables the convolution operation to adjust its 
position on the image adaptively, better capturing the local structure 
and semantic information of Barnyard Grass, thus overcoming the po­
sitional constraints of traditional convolution. Experimental results 
show that after introducing deformable convolution, the model’s accu­
racy significantly increased from 58.2 % to 61.4 %, an improvement of 
3.5 %. This demonstrates that introducing deformable convolution in 
the decoding stage can effectively enhance the model’s weed recogni­
tion accuracy.

6. Conclusion
This paper introduces a rice-weed segmentation method based on the 
fusion of CNN and Transformer features ——CTFFNet. The motivation 
behind this approach stems from the need to address different weeds in 
rice fields, with CNN and Transformer having their respective strengths. 
Therefore, we chose to combine CNN and Transformer feature extrac­
tion modules to comprehensively capture rich features from rice field 
remote sensing images and global contextual relationships. In the 
feature fusion stage, we employ the CBAM module. Experimental results 
demonstrate that compared to simple convolutional feature fusion, the 
CBAM module can increase the MIoU by 0.2 %. To address the issue of 
misalignment of weed features in the feature maps, we used deformable 
convolution for upsampling in the decoding stage. The results show that 
the application of deformable convolution in the decoding phase 
significantly improves the model’s segmentation accuracy, increasing 
accuracy by 3.5 %.
In future work, we plan to deploy this deep learning model on a crop 
protection drone platform with the aim of achieving real-time identifi­
cation of rice field weeds and generating variable spraying prescriptions. 
This will meet to the requirements of precision spraying applications in 
rice fields.
