I. INTRODUCTION 
Weeds are opportunistic plants that invade crop fields and 
severely reduce yields by competing with crops for water, 
nutrients, light and physical space [1]. Conventional weed 
management is mainly based on mechanical operations such 
as tillage or mowing, combined with the large-scale 
application of chemical herbicides [2]. Although these 
strategies are effective in the short term, the environmental 
footprint of herbicides and their potential health risks have 
driven a growing interest in more sustainable weed-control 
solutions [3-4]. 
In this context, recent progress in UAV-based imaging 
and deep learning has opened promising avenues for 
automated, site-specific weed detection. Unmanned aerial 
vehicles and agricultural robots, coupled with vision 
algorithms, can detect and map weeds with high spatial 
precision, enabling targeted interventions and reducing 
overall herbicide usage [4-5]. Within this framework, 
semantic segmentation plays a central role: by predicting a 
class label for every pixel, it produces detailed plant masks 
that are directly exploitable for applications such as 
precision spraying or mechanical removal [6]. 
Nonetheless, robust weed identification inside dense crop 
canopies remains a difficult problem. In standard RGB 
images, weeds often resemble the crop in terms of color, 
texture and overall morphology, which frequently leads to 
pixel-level confusion between the two classes [7]. 
Furthermore, the visual appearance of plants in the field is 
strongly influenced by acquisition conditions: fluctuations in 
illumination, cast shadows, motion blur and occlusions can 
all distort perceived colors and contrasts, thereby degrading 
the stability of segmentation models [7-8]. This challenge is 
compounded by the high morphological diversity of weed 
species and by their changing appearance across growth 
stages and environmental contexts, which makes it difficult 
for a single model to generalize across different fields and 
seasons [9]. Moreover, single-branch U-Net models 
typically process all input features within a single stream, 
which may limit their ability to disentangle visually similar 
crops and weeds and often requires large annotated datasets 
and substantial computational resources, making real-time 
deployment in the field more challenging [10]. 
 
 
To overcome the limitations of purely RGB-based 
approaches, this work exploits richer spectral information 
combined with vegetation indices. The input data include 
visible bands (R, G, B), near-infrared (NIR) and red-edge 
(RE), from which five vegetation indices are derived: 
NDVI, EVI, MSAVI, NDRE and GNDVI. While NIR and 
RE are sensitive to leaf internal structure, chlorophyll 
content and canopy density, the indices summarize this 
multispectral information into biophysically meaningful 
descriptors of greenness, vigor and stress. Together, they 
enhance the contrast between crop and weeds in situations 
where both appear very similar in RGB, for example under 
non-uniform illumination, shadows or heterogeneous soil 
backgrounds. An important question addressed in this study 
is whether these vegetation indices still provide a significant 
benefit when NIR and RE bands are already available, or 
whether raw multispectral imagery alone would be sufficient 
for robust weed–crop segmentation.

II. RELATED WORKS 
A. U-Net in Agriculture 
U-Net is an encoder–decoder fully convolutional network 
widely adopted for semantic segmentation in remote sensing 
and agricultural imaging due to its efficiency and strong 
localization ability [11-12]. Bragagnolo et al. [13] applied 
U-Net for Amazon forest cover mapping and reported 
94.7% overall accuracy, illustrating its effectiveness in 
delineating fragmented regions. Several variants improve 
feature learning and boundary delineation, including Sharp 
U-Net for finer feature fusion [14] and residual designs that 
enhance representation capacity and gradient flow [15-17]. 
However, segmentation in complex vegetation scenes 
especially with high-dimensional spectral information can 
increase computation and may still suffer from edge artifacts 
or class confusion, motivating improved architectures and 
feature-fusion strategies [15-17]. 
B. Dual-Branch U-Net for Semantic Segmentation 
To better exploit complementary cues, dual-branch U-
Nets introduce specialized streams and fusion mechanisms. 
Wang et al. [18] proposed a dual-branch design for OCTA 
vessel segmentation that combines branches tailored to fine 
details and high-level semantics. Dual-stream formulations 
have also been explored beyond standard semantic cues, 
such as DMU-Net for splicing forgery localization, which 
fuses an RGB stream with a noise-oriented stream to 
improve localization [19]. In plant-related applications, Yu 
et al. [20] separated PCA-compressed spectral information 
from handcrafted descriptors within a dual-way U-Net for 
vegetation classification, while Zhang et al. [21] integrated 
image and spectral branches with hierarchical fusion for 
crop classification.  
More broadly, recent dual-branch models in precision 
agriculture support the benefit of combining local and global 
representations, including CNN–Transformer fusion for 
UAV based disease detection [22] and other auxiliary-
branch designs for mapping and delineation tasks [23-24], 
[25]. These studies motivate investigating branched U-Net 
designs to better disentangle visually similar classes and 
exploit complementary feature spaces. 
C. Multispectral Imaging for Crop-Weed Segmentation 
Multispectral imagery (RGB with near-infrared (NIR) and 
often red-edge (RE)) provides physically meaningful cues 
related to canopy structure and chlorophyll content, and is 
frequently combined with vegetation indices (VIs) to 
improve robustness under illumination changes, shadows, 
and heterogeneous backgrounds. Index-based descriptors 
have also been used as non-destructive indicators of plant 
status in close-range RGB imaging (e.g., NGRDI), 
supporting the broader relevance of VIs as interpretable 
plant-status cues [26]. Wang et al. [27] presented a UAV-
based weed-detection pipeline that fuses multispectral bands 
and multiple VIs and reported strong performance with a 
ResNet backbone, highlighting the utility of spectral 
features for field-scale weed mapping. Sahin et al. [28] 
showed that combining visible and NIR information with an 
index channel can further improve early-stage weed 
segmentation. Celikkan et al. [29] introduced WeedsGalore, 
a multispectral and multitemporal UAV benchmark for 
crop–weed segmentation in maize fields that provides 
aligned RGB, NIR, and red-edge imagery with dense 
crop/weed annotations captured across multiple acquisition 
dates; their baselines show consistent gains when NIR/RE 
are included compared with RGB-only inputs.  
Additional 
studies 
also 
report

segmentation and vegetation extraction [30-31]. Overall, 
while multispectral bands especially NIR and RE improve 
crop–weed segmentation, most approaches still process 
channels in a single stream, motivating our investigation of 
multi-branch and hybrid CNN–Transformer U-Nets and our 
analysis of whether VI channels remain beneficial when 
high quality NIR/RE bands are available. 
III. METHODOLOGY 
In this section, we describe the complete workflow used 
for UAV-based crop weed semantic segmentation, from 
input preparation to model evaluation. The methodology is 
designed to study how architectural choices (single-branch 
vs. multi-branch U-Net variants) and spectral enrichment 
(multispectral bands and vegetation indices) influence 
segmentation 
performance 
under 
challenging 
field 
conditions such as illumination changes, shadows, and soil 
background variability.

This work designs a family of U-Net based architectures 
[32] for UAV crop weed segmentation, combining 
convolutional and Transformer encoders and varying the 
number of branches from a single pathway to dual and multi 
branch configurations. All models are built on relatively 
lightweight backbones, namely ResNet-34 and Swin-T.

1) Single branch U-unets (SU-R, SU-S) 
The first baselines are single-branch U-Nets that process 
all input channels within a single encoder–decoder stream. 
In the SU-R model, the encoder is a ResNet-34 network. As 
the feature maps pass through successive residual blocks, 
the spatial resolution is reduced while the number of 
channels increases, with typical feature map sizes of 64 
channels at H/4×W/4, 128 channels at H/8×W/8, 256 
channels at H/16×W/16 and 512 channels at H/32×W/32 
[33]. The decoder mirrors this pyramid, progressively 
upsampling the feature maps back to the original image 
resolution and reducing the channel dimensionality to 256, 
then 128, and finally 64 channels before the final prediction 
layer. Standard U-Net skip connections link each encoder 
level to the corresponding decoder level, allowing the model 
to preserve fine spatial details such as leaf contours, thin 
stems, small gaps between plants and isolated weed patches. 
In this configuration, ResNet-34 acts as a local feature 
extractor with convolutional receptive fields that favour 
detailed texture and edge information. 
The SU-S model shares the same U-Net decoder structure 
but replaces the encoder with a Swin-T Transformer. After 
patch embedding and hierarchical downsampling, the Swin-
T encoder produces four stages of feature maps with 96, 
192, 384 and 768 channels at resolutions H/4×W/4, 
H/8×W/8, H/16×W/16 and H/32×W/32, respectively [34]. 
These representations are computed through windowed self-
attention and shifted windows [35], where each patch 
attends to all other patches within its window using data-
dependent weights. In contrast to fixed convolution kernels, 
self-attention dynamically aggregates information from the 
most relevant regions, allowing the network to relate 
spectrally similar but spatially distant structures. By 
stacking multiple Swin-T blocks with shifted windows, 
information gradually flows across neighboring windows, so 
that the encoder builds a global view of the canopy while 
preserving fine local details. This mechanism is particularly 
valuable for crop–weed segmentation, as it enables the 
model to jointly exploit local leaf textures and long-range 
cues such as row organization, large weed clusters, and 
contextual differences between crop plants and surrounding 
vegetation. To make the comparison with SU-R fair and to 
reuse a common decoder design, the Swin-T feature maps 
are passed through 1×1 convolutions that project them down 
to 64, 128, 256 and 512 channels, matching the ResNet-34 
pyramid.  
The projected features are then fed to the same decoder 
(with 256, 128 and 64 channels on the way back to full 
resolution), so that any performance differences can be 
attributed to the encoder type rather than to the decoder 
capacity. Fig. 1 illustrates this single branch U-Net template 
with a single encoder and a single decoder connected 
through lateral skip connections.

3) Multi-Branch U-Net (MBU) 
MBU extends the dual-branch strategy to four branches in 
order to explicitly separate spectral bands and vegetation 
indices. In the proposed design, two branches process the 
raw spectral bands (RGB + NIR + RE) and two branches 
process the vegetation indices. Practically, two of the 
branches are implemented with ResNet-34 encoders, and the 
other two with Swin-T encoders. As in the dual-branch case, 
all encoder outputs are projected to a common set of channel 
widths (64, 128, 256 and 512) at the four spatial scales. This 
guarantees that each branch contributes with a similar 
capacity at fusion time and that the total model size remains 
compatible with the available GPU memory.

C. Data Acquisition 
 Data acquisition 
In this study, we build on the WeedsGalore dataset 
introduced by Celikkan et al. [29], which provides high-
resolution UAV imagery for weed detection in maize fields. 
The experimental field is located near Potsdam, Germany, 
and covers approximately 1840 m². Maize (Zea mays L.) 
was sown with a row spacing of 70 cm and an intra-row 
spacing of 20 cm. Weeds emerged naturally under standard 
farming practices, without experimental interfere
sulting in a realistic mixture of species and densities. 
Multispectral and RGB images were acquired using a DJI 
Phantom 4 Multispectral platform equipped with five 
narrow-band monochrome sensors (Blue 450±16 nm, Green 
560±16 nm, Red 650±16 nm, Red-Edge 730±16 nm, Near-
Infrared 840±26 nm) and a separate RGB camera. Flights 
were planned with high overlap (≈ 70 % side, 60 % front) 
and conducted at a height of 5 m in Hover & Capture mode, 
yielding a ground sampling distance of about 2.5 mm. Four 
acquisition campaigns were carried out on May 25, May 30, 
June 6 and June 15, covering early maize emergence (V1 
stage) up to almost full canopy cover, and therefore

V. DISCUSSION 
The experimental results highlight the central role of 
hybrid dual-branch architectures in achieving accurate crop–
weed segmentation under realistic field conditions. Among 
all configurations, the DBU-RS model with RGB+NIR+ RE 
input provides the best overall trade-off between accuracy, 
robustness and computational cost, with a Dice score of 
91.73 %, and an IoU of 85.22 %. These gains can be directly 
linked to the complementary nature of its two encoder 
branches: the ResNet34 backbone focuses on local spatial 
patterns (leaf contours, fine gaps between plants, small weed 
clusters), while the Swin-T encoder, through windowed self-
attention, captures global contextual relationships such as 
row structure, larger weed patches and broader canopy 
organization. By fusing these two types of information at 
multiple scales in the decoder, DBU-RS is able to 
simultaneously preserve sharp object boundaries and 
maintain a coherent global under
hich is crucial for distinguishing crops from weeds in 
dense and heterogeneous canopies.

is that the feature map dimensionality of each branch is 
carefully balanced. At every encoder stage, the ResNet34 
and Swin-T branches are adjusted so that their feature maps 
have compatible spatial resolutions and similar channel 
depths before concatenation. This prevents one branch from 
producing feature vectors that are much “larger” or more 
dominant than the other. If the Transformer branch had
