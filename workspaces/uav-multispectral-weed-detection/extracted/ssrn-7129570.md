ABSTRACT
13
Invasive aquatic weeds, particularly water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes), pose a major threat 
14
to freshwater ecosystems, causing significant ecological and economic damage in Australia. 
15
Accurate detection and mapping are essential for effective and timely management. However, 
16
conventional satellite and airborne multispectral methods are often limited by insufficient 
17
spatial resolution and spectral ambiguity in complex riparian environments. This study 
18
evaluated UAV-based RGB and multispectral data for water hyacinth detection in the Fitzroy 
19
River Basin, central Queensland, Australia, and proposed a Hybrid Deep Refinement Network 
20
(HDR-Net) for instance segmentation. UAV imagery was acquired at 20 m above ground level 
21
using a DJI Mavic 3 Multispectral platform, achieving a ground sampling distance of 0.7 
22
cm/pixel. Multispectral indices were assessed for vegetation–water discrimination. Results 
23
showed that RGB-derived indices provide stronger spatial separability, while multispectral 
24
indices were constrained by spectral similarity among vegetation types and on water surfaces. 
25
HDR-Net combined a lightweight YOLOv8n segmentation model with a U-Net++ refinement 
26
branch in a two-stage pipeline. YOLOv8n first performed region-of-interest localisation, and 
27
U-Net++ refined pixel-level segmentation within these regions, reducing background 
28
interference and false positives. On a held-out test set of 400 UAV image tiles, HDR-Net 
29
achieved a Dice score of 0.9463, precision of 0.9580, recall of 0.9610, and mAP@50 of 0.924, 
30
outperforming standalone baselines. The false positive rate (4.2%) and false negative rate

Introduction
39
1.1
The Threat of Invasive Aquatic Weeds in the Fitzroy River Basin
40
Invasive aquatic weeds are considered as one of the most significant threats to freshwater 
41
ecosystem globally (Gallardo et al., 2016). In Australia, invasive and certain native plant 
42
species impact cropping, livestock, and public land management systems. These impacts result 
43
in estimated annual losses exceeding AUD 7.1 billion (Llewellyn et al., 2016, Sinden et al., 
44
2004). The issue is further worsened by the continued deliberate and accidental introduction of 
45
exotic species (Groves et al., 2003, Sindel and Coleman, 2010). Although Australian 
46
government responses to invasive flora date back to 1903, effective landscape-scale control 
47
remains an ongoing challenge (Cullen et al., 2023).
48
49
The Fitzroy River Basin, covering approximately 143,000 km² in central Queensland, supports 
50
one of the most biodiverse assemblages of flora and fauna in the region. This area provides 
51
habitat for 5,877 native species and 192 plant taxa classified as rare or endangered (Flint et al., 
52
2017). These conservation values are under direct threat from three invasive aquatic 
53
macrophytes: water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes), Salvinia (Salvinia molesta), and water 
54
lettuce (Pistia stratiotes), and their shapes and infestation patterns are shown in Figure 1. These 
55
species alter vegetation structure and ecological processes, compete with native flora, degrade 
56
habitat for native fauna, reduce dissolved oxygen through decomposition, impede navigation, 
57
and obstruct irrigation infrastructure (Rodriguez-Garlito et al., 2022, Retnamma et al., 2023, 
58
Shen et al., 2025, Di Lernia et al., 2025, Henny et al., 2026). In response to these threats, the 
59
Rockhampton Regional Council Biosecurity Plan 2022–2026 ranked management of invasive 
60
aquatic weeds as the second highest priority among seven key biosecurity concerns. Therefore,

Remote sensing has emerged as an attractive tool for non-invasive, large-scale aquatic

Satellite platforms particularly the Sentinel-2 (Drusch et al., 2012) and Landsat series 
73
(Matongera et al., 2017) offer broad spatial coverage, frequent revisit cycles, and long-term 
74
archives that facilitate temporal monitoring of aquatic vegetation dynamics across extensive 
75
areas. In 2008, Lawes and Wallace (Lawes and Wallace, 2008) used spectral indices derived 
76
from Landsat images to map and monitor prickly acacia density across 29,000 km² of Mitchell 
77
grass plains in northern Australia, successfully identifying its spatial distribution and providing 
78
a basis for assessing its spread and informing management strategies. Furthermore, recent 
79
studies have demonstrated that deep learning can further enhance the utility of satellite data 
80
(Etienne et al., 2021). For instance, Rodríguez-Garlito et al.  (Rodriguez-Garlito et al., 2022) 
81
applied a convolutional neural network (CNN) to Sentinel-2 imagery for aquatic weed 
82
detection in the Guadiana River, Spain, achieving overall accuracies of 77.9–90.3% across two 
83
study sites. 
84
85
Nevertheless, satellite remote sensing still faces persistent limitations for fine-scale monitoring 
86
in complex riparian environments. The free Sentinel-2 imagery has a spatial resolution of 10 
87
m per pixel, while commercial very high resolution (VHR) imagery achieves submeter 
88
resolution; in contrast, aquatic weed monitoring often requires centimeter-level resolution. 
89
Coarse spatial resolution constrains the detection of sparse or early-stage infestations. In 
90
addition, surface reflectance, water turbidity, cloud cover, and atmospheric interference further 
91
degrade classification accuracy, while fixed revisit schedules limit responsiveness to rapidly 
92
changing weed conditions caused by water flow (Hestir et al., 2015, Dronova, 2015, Alemneh 
93
et al., 2025).
94
95
UAVs can overcome many of these limitations by enabling on-demand and low-altitude 
96
deployment. These platforms can provide very high spatial resolutions, often achieving sub-
97
centimetre ground sampling distances (GSD) at flight heights of approximately 10 m above 
98
ground level (AGL). Such fine spatial detail enables the detection of individual plants and 
99
early-stage infestations that are typically undetectable using satellite imaging (Toscano et al., 
100
2024). A wide range of sensors can be integrated with UAV platforms. Among them, standard 
101
RGB cameras are particularly effective for capturing visually distinct features and dense 
102
canopy structures (Tait et al., 2019). Multispectral sensors can enhance discriminability by 
103
incorporating red-edge and near-infrared bands, supporting the computation of vegetation

105

106
et al., 2018). UAV platforms also offer high operational flexibility in terms of flight scheduling 
107
and revisit frequency, while maintaining relatively low acquisition and operational costs 
108

Despite their advantages, UAV-based imaging systems are limited by restricted spatial

1.3
Deep Learning in UAV-Based Weed Detection
114
Deep learning has revolutionised UAV-based weed detection by hierarchical feature learning 
115
from image data, overcoming the limitations of hand-crafted spectral and textural features in 
116
complex vegetation environments. Object detection frameworks, particularly the YOLO family 
117
(Redmon et al., 2016) and R-CNN variants (Girshick, 2015), have demonstrated strong weed 
118
detection performance. In a study by (Cui et al., 2024), various deep learning models were 
119
applied to UAV imagery for weed detection in soybean fields; Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2015) 
120
achieved an average precision of 88.69% mAP, while YOLOv8 (Glenn Jocher et al., 2023) 
121
achieved 85.77% mAP.
122
123
Another important application is the estimation of weed distribution to assess the extent of 
124
infestation. Therefore, precise segmentation of weeds from the background is essential. 
125
Semantic segmentation models have demonstrated strong performance in pixel-wise weed 
126
segmentation and quantification tasks. To address the challenges of weed identification and 
127
quantification in potato fields, Sandoval-Pillajo et al. (Sandoval-Pillajo et al., 2026) evaluated 
128
several U-Net variants for automated semantic segmentation using low-altitude UAV imagery. 
129
The UAV operated at a flight height of 9–10 m with a speed of 1 m/s. The results showed that 
130
residual U-Net achieved the best performance, obtaining a mean Intersection over Union 
131
(mIoU) of 0.8021. This demonstrated its robustness of deep learning for accurate weed 
132
segmentation and site-specific quantification in precision agriculture applications. Jin et al. (Jin 
133
et al., 2022) proposed an improved Mask R-CNN model by incorporating a Convolutional 
134
Block Attention Module (CBAM), achieving a reported mean Average Precision (mAP) of 
135
0.948 for segmentation of small weeds in beet crops. To enable spot spraying and reduce 
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=7129570
Preprint not peer reviewed


136
herbicide usage in agriculture, Allmendinger et al. (Allmendinger et al., 2025) compared two 
137
major deep learning paradigms, YOLO-based detectors and transformer-based models, for 
138
detecting 16 weed species. The results showed that YOLOv9 achieved a mAP@50 of 79.86%, 
139
while the transformer-based DETR achieved a slightly higher mAP@50 of 81.46%. Although 
140
DETR demonstrated superior detection accuracy, it suffered longer inference times.
141
142
The study in (Machidon et al., 2025) introduced SSU-Net, an adaptive deep learning model for 
143
real-time image segmentation on resource-constrained UAVs. It combined U-Net, SqueezeNet, 
144
and slimmable neural networks. The model dynamically adjusted its network width based on 
145
task demands and battery constraints, but still achieved comparable performance to state-of-
146
the-art models, with approximately 90%–91% accuracy. It also used significantly fewer 
147
parameters, enabling real-time processing.
148
149
These studies demonstrate strong potential in applying deep learning for weed detection using 
150
drone imagery in agricultural settings. They provide a solid methodological foundation for 
151
semantic segmentation-based approaches based on low-altitude UAV data. However, 
152
extending these deep learning methods to aquatic environments introduces additional 
153
challenges. These include specular water surface reflections and morphological similarity 
154
between weeds and native aquatic vegetation. As a result, more careful dataset design, data 
155
augmentation, and potentially well-designed deep neural network architectures are required.

1.4
Research Contributions and Scope
157
This study addressed the challenge of accurate detection and segmentation of aquatic weeds in 
158
complex riparian environments using UAV remote sensing and deep learning. The main 
159
contributions are summarised as follows.
160
1) We conducted a systematic comparison of UAV RGB imagery, UAV-derived 
161
multispectral indices (NDVI, NDRE, OSAVI), and airborne multispectral data for 
162
water hyacinth detection in the Fitzroy River Basin. Results showed that low-altitude 
163
RGB imagery outperformed multispectral imaging due to higher spatial resolution and 
164
stronger texture and colour contrast. 
165
2) We present an optimised end-to-end UAV monitoring workflow that integrates a hybrid 
166
detection–segmentation deep learning approach. The approach combines YOLOv8n

The study area is located within the Fitzroy River catchment in central Queensland, Australia 
185
(23°10′55″S, 150°23′01″E; Figure 2). The Fitzroy River is one of the largest river systems in 
186
Queensland and represents a critical freshwater ecosystem of high ecological and hydrological 
187

188

189

190

191
192
Effective management of this infestation requires accurate and timely spatial detection across 
193
the river’s extensive and often inaccessible riparian zones. In practice, aerial spraying via 
194
helicopter is currently one of the primary viable methods for treating large-scale infestations in 
195
upstream sections, particularly where access by boat is not possible. However, precise 
196
estimation of water hyacinth extent is essential to minimise herbicide overuse, as excessive 
197
herbicide usage can harm aquatic fauna and contribute to chemical pollution.
198
199
Therefore, this study developed and evaluated a deep learning-based detection framework 
200
using low-altitude RGB imagery acquired via UAVs, and benchmarked it against other remote 
201
sensing approaches such as airborne platforms (e.g., manned aircraft). The aim is to assess the 
202
comparative effectiveness of these approaches for high-resolution mapping of water hyacinth 
203
across the Fitzroy River Basin and to support more efficient and targeted weed management 
204
strategies.

2.4
Hybrid Deep Refinement Network for Water Hyacinth Segmentation
242
Although UAV imagery has high spatial resolution, water hyacinth is still challenging to detect 
243
due to strong water surface reflectance, illumination variations, and spectral similarity with 
244
surrounding vegetation and water–vegetation boundary effects. By reviewing the performance 
245
of several popular segmentation models, this study proposes a Hybrid Deep Refinement 
246
Network (HDR-Net), consisting of a lightweight YOLOv8n (Glenn Jocher et al., 2023) 
247
detection architecture and a U-Net++ segmentation model. The proposed HDR-Net addresses 
248
the detection–segmentation gap between object localisation and fine-grained segmentation. 
249
Object-level localisation was first performed using YOLOv8n. Context-aware semantic 
250
refinement was subsequently applied using U-Net++. This sequential design leveraged the 
251
strengths of both architectures. YOLOv8n provided precise instance-level localisation, while

Preprint not peer reviewed

U-Net++ refined spatial detail and improved boundary delineation. The technical details of 
253

2.5

The YOLOv8 block (Figure 4) served as the first stage of HDR-Net, responsible for rapid 
261
instance localisation and coarse segmentation of water hyacinth regions. The backbone adopted 
262
a C2F (Cross-Stage Partial with Fused Two Convolutions) block, which split the input features 
263
into two groups: one passed through a series of bottleneck layers to capture fine-grained 
264
textural details, while the other bypassed further processing to preserve broader spatial context. 
265
All intermediate outputs were subsequently concatenated, enabling efficient multi-level feature 
266
extraction. This was particularly beneficial for distinguishing water hyacinth from similar 
267
surrounding vegetation and water–vegetation boundaries. Rather than applying multiple large 
268
pooling kernels in parallel, the SPPF (Spatial Pyramid Pooling – Fast) module achieved multi-
269
scale context aggregation by sequentially applying a small 5×5 max-pooling kernel three times, 
270
which is faster than the original SPP formulation. This multi-scale pooling was especially 
271
advantageous for handling the variable canopy sizes and fragmented spatial distributions 
272
characteristic of water hyacinth infestations in UAV imagery. Together, these components 
273
enhanced feature extraction, promoted feature reuse, and reduced computational bottlenecks.

2.11 Multispectral Index Analysis
406
UAV orthomosaic analysis was performed using colour-based spectral indices for initial land 
407
cover discrimination. The Green Excess Index (GEI = G − (R + B)/2) was used for RGB-based 
408
vegetation detection, as it captured green canopy dominance. The Excess Blue Index (EBI = B 
409
− (R + G)/2) was applied to identify open water regions based on enhanced blue reflectance. A 
410
thresholding strategy was adopted for pixel-wise classification. Pixels with GEI > 10 were 
411
classified as dense vegetation, while values in the range 0 ≤ GEI ≤ 10 were labelled as sparse 
412
or mixed vegetation. Pixels with EBI > 5 were classified as open water.
413
414
In addition, multispectral vegetation indices were generated from the UAV data, including 
415
NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), NDRE (Normalized Difference Red Edge 
416
Index), and OSAVI (Optimised Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index). These indices were evaluated 
417
for their effectiveness in distinguishing aquatic vegetation from surrounding water and for 
418
supporting water hyacinth mapping in complex wetland environments.

4
Discussion and Future Research
528
This study showed that low-altitude RGB UAV imagery outperformed multispectral remote 
529
sensing for water hyacinth detection in complex riparian environments. Multispectral indices 
530
(NDVI, NDRE, OSAVI) can separate vegetation from open water but struggled to distinguish 
531
water hyacinth from spectrally similar vegetation. This was mainly due to spectral saturation 
532
among green vegetation, reduced NIR response in dense floating mats, and mixed-pixel effects 
533
at water–vegetation boundaries.
534
535
In contrast, RGB-derived indices (GEI and EBI) provided better separation between vegetation 
536
and non-vegetation. They relied on colour intensity and texture rather than spectral ratios. Their 
537
performance was further supported by higher spatial resolution (GSD ≈ 0.7 cm/pixel at 20 m 
538
AGL), which captured fine structures such as rosette patterns, canopy gaps, and fragmented 
539
vegetation mat edges that were not resolved in multispectral or high-altitude airborne imagery.
540
541
The proposed HDR-Net achieved superior performance over YOLOv8-Seg and U-Net++ 
542
baselines, particularly in mAP@50 (+0.030 to +0.042) and precision improvements. Recall 
543
remained comparable across models (0.9607–0.9633), indicating similar detection coverage. 
544
The main gain included reduced false positives. These issues arose from water reflections, 
545
debris, and spectrally similar vegetation. The two-stage design was playing the key role. 
546
YOLOv8n provided ROI localisation, and U-Net++ refines segmentation within constrained 
547
regions. This ROI constraint improved boundary quality and reduced misclassification, as 
548
shown by errors in standalone U-Net++ results. Training stability was improved using a warm-
549
up strategy with gradient isolation. Early loss fluctuations stabilized by epoch 10. This 
550
suggested effective feature alignment between fused architectures.
551
552
Future work should extend multi-season datasets to improve robustness. Multispectral fusion 
553
with RGB could enhance species-level discrimination. Lightweight model compression could 
554
enable onboard deployment. Multi-class extension to other invasive species e.g., salvinia and 
555
water lettuce is also recommended. Finally, integration with geospatial information systems 
556
(GIS) and herbicide optimisation models would improve operational deployment for large-
557
scale weed management.

5
Conclusions
560
This study evaluated the effectiveness of UAV-based RGB and multispectral remote sensing 
561
for water hyacinth detection and proposed a Hybrid Deep Refinement Network (HDR-Net) for 
562
instance segmentation in the Fitzroy River Basin, central Queensland.
563
564
 Our findings demonstrated that low-altitude RGB UAV imagery (20 m AGL, GSD ≈ 0.7 
565
cm/pixel) outperformed multispectral data for fine-scale water hyacinth mapping. 
566
Multispectral indices (NDVI, NDRE, OSAVI) enabled general vegetation–water separation 
567
but were limited in distinguishing water hyacinth from other vegetation due to spectral 
568
similarity among green vegetation, NIR attenuation in dense floating mats, and mixed-pixel 
569
effects at boundaries. Airborne imagery (~1000 m AGL) lacked sufficient spatial resolution 
570
for detailed weed delineation.
571
572
The proposed HDR-Net achieved the best overall performance for water hyacinth segmentation, 
573
with a Dice score of 0.9463, precision of 0.9580, recall of 0.9610, and mAP@50 of 0.924. Its 
574
two-stage design, combining YOLOv8n-based localisation with U-Net++ refinement and ROI-
575
constrained suppression, effectively reduced false positives and improved boundary accuracy 
576
compared to standalone models. The proposed framework achieved a false positive rate of 4.2% 
577
and a false negative rate of 3.9%, indicating reliable infestation delineation.  By enabling highly 
578
accurate, automated mapping, this two-stage pipeline provides an operational tool for precision 
579
biosecurity, allowing catchment managers to optimize targeted herbicide application and 
580
prioritise critical treatment zones.
