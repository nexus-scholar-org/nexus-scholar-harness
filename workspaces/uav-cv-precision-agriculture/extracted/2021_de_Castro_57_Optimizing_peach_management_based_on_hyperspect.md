## Abstract

The purpose of this research was to examine and monitor factors affecting peach cultivation as a part  of an overall research program for developing Integrated Pest Management strategies. Potassium  fertilization and the practice of root-collar excavation have proven effective in increasing yield and  fruit quality and in extending orchard longevity on infested replant sites, respectively. Remote sensing  has been proven to be an effective technology for monitoring crop conditions at field scale. Thus,  hyperspectral imagery collected from a UAV was used to assess the effect on the peach spectral  signature of two foliar potassium spray rates and two soil management treatments as well as the  damage produced by peach rust. Significant differences in peach canopy hyperspectral profiles were  observed in all analysis performed, i.e. according to the K-treatments; to the soil managements; and  to the severity classes. In addition, the image wavebands that efficiently discriminated the evaluated  factors were identified.

Keywords: crop protection, integrated pest management, agricultural practices, peach rust, remote  sensing, image analysis, vegetation index


## Introduction

Peach is the main fruit tree in the southeastern US with 32% of the total peach bearing area in the  U.S. (USDA-NASS, 2020). Peach trees are often fertilized without considering tree requirements  (Zhou and Melgar, 2019), and excessive application of nutrients are commonly seen in commercial  orchards, which can lead to internal canopy shading and reduced fruit quality (Layne, 2011). Peach rust can have a significant impact on the productivity of stone fruits, including peach trees. The  disease is caused by Tranzschelia discolor, a fungus that overwinters in woody tissue (Soto-Estrada  et al., 2005). Infectious spores are moved by wind and rainstorms to leaves where they produce  yellow lesions. If infections are severe, the tree will shed leaves early. That prevents the tree from  translocating nutrient resources from the leaves to the roots. Year after year early defoliation will  weaken the tree and productivity suffers. Management is complicated by the fact that growers do  not typically spray pesticides after the crop is harvested for economic reasons. Diagnostic methods consisting of visual inspection, collection of samples, and laboratory analyses  are costly and time-consuming. Remote sensing has an enormous potential for monitoring the  health status of crops, since it allows the rapid and reliable collection and processing of the data  (Zhang et al., 2019). Remote sensing of disease detection based on UAV imagery and hyperspectral  information is a desirable option to assist enhance or replace manual surveys. While there are many  studies using multispectral aerial imaging to monitor and quantify disease in crops, such as cotton  (Wang et al. 2020), avocado (de Castro et al., 2015) and grapevine (Albetis et al., 2017), there is  no widespread use of UAV-based hyperspectral imaging for this purpose, in spite of the fact that

it is currently considered a cutting-edge technology. The main reason is the greater complexity in  image pre-processing than for traditional red, green and blue (RGB) or multispectral imaging. On  the other hand, hyperspectral sensors acquire hundreds of narrow and contiguous spectral bands  from visible to the infra-red range of the electromagnetic spectrum, compared with the smaller  number and wider bands of multispectral images. Hyperspectral sensors carried in a UAV have  been used to detect yellow rust in wheat (Bohnenkamp et al., 2019) and huanglongbing in citrus  (Deng et al., 2020). This research is part of an overall research program to develop Integrated Pest Management  (IPM) strategies in peach crops. In this program, several management strategies to improve peach  management are investigated, e.g. nutrient application, soil management and disease control. For  nutrient application, potassium (K) foliar fertilization is considered a key factor for yield, diameter  and weight fruit. In the case of soil management, the practice of root-collar excavation, which  involves permanent removal of the soil from the base of the trunk, has been shown to improve yield  and prevent the progression of tree mortality on Armillaria root rot infected replant sites (Miller et  al., 2020). The purpose of this research is to investigate the potential of UAV-based hyperspectral  imagery to examine and monitor the factors affecting the peach growth. The specific objectives  consisted of: (1) establishing a protocol for image pre-processing and retrieving the peach canopy  hyperspectral signature values; (2) quantifying the influence of different doses of K application as well  as the use of root-collar excavation in the spectral signature of peach trees; and (3) determining the  variations in the spectral signature caused by peach rust and selecting the best 1nm-wide wavebands  to efficiently discriminate affected trees at different development stages.

Materials and methods

Study and preparation area The experiment was carried out in an experimental orchard of 85 peach trees at Clemson University´s  Musser Fruit Research Center, located in Seneca, (34.601° N, -82.873 E, WGS84) (South Carolina,  USA), representative of the growing practices in the region. Experimental five-year-old ‘PF-23’  peach trees on Guardian® rootstock, trained to the perpendicular-V system, and planted on a sandy  loam soil at a distance of 1.5 m between trees and 6 m between rows, were used. The experiment  consisted of five rows of 17 trees each and followed a split-plot design with two factors: K fertilization  and soil management. Three rows were fertilized with the grower standard K rate (1.0 kg K/ha)  while two rows received 5× the standard K rate. Potassium fertilizer was applied by hand to the  soil directly underneath the tree canopy. Half of trees in each row followed grower standard soil  management (non-excavated), whereas the other half underwent root collar excavation (excavated),  as described previously by Miller et al. (2020). In brief, four combined treatments were carried out:  non-excavated/1 K, non-excavated/5 K, excavated/1 K and excavated/5 K. The presence of the peach rust disease was assessed by the degree of defoliation and then, confirmed  via laboratory analysis. Four different stages of disease development, based on the defoliation level,  were found in the experiment: healthy (non-defoliated), low (defoliated <20%), medium (defoliated  20-50%), and high (defoliated >50%) (Figure 1).

Image acquisition The aerial images were taken in September 2019 with a quadrocopter UAV platform with vertical  take-off and landing, model DJI Matrice 600 Pro with Ronin-MX Gimbal (DJI Science and  Technology Co. Ltd., Shenzhen, China), equipped with a Senop HSC-2 hyperspectral Camera  (Senop Oy, Oulu, Finland). This camera takes images in a spectral range of 500-900 nm with a  bandwidth of 1.0 nm, 1 megapixel resolution (1,024×1,024 pixels) without using interpolation,  and 12 bits of radiometric resolution. The UAV flew at 50 m altitude, which resulted in a spatial  resolution of 5 cm/pixel.

Image pre-processing: mosaicking and radiometric calibration The set of UAV images was processed to create an ortho-mosaicked image using Agisoft Photoscan  Professional Edition (Agisoft LLC, St. Petersburg, Russia) software following the protocol described  in de Castro et al. (2018), which consisted of three steps: aligning images, building field geometry,  and ortho-photo generation. The process was fully automatic with the only exception of the manual  localization of 6 ground control points. The final product was the ortho-mosaicked image that  covered the entire field where each pixel corresponded to individual 203 bands spectral information  and Cartesian coordinates. Radiometric correction was carried out using the empirical line calibration (Smith & Milton, 1999)  implemented in ENVI software (ENVI, Research Systems Inc., Boulder, CO, USA) to fit digital  values of the hyperspectral imagery to the target reflectance spectra. The target reflectance were  obtained from two calibration targets used during the flight with an average reflectance of 2% for  the black and 83% for the white one. The result was an image where each pixel presented digital  counts within the range of 0-100% reflectance values.

Object-based image analysis A customized algorithm based on object-based image analysis (OBIA) paradigm was developed to  automatically isolate each peach tree and to retrieve its spectral information. The algorithm is fully  automatic and self-adaptive to any study plot of this experiment. Cognition Network programming  language with eCognition Developer 9 software (Trimble GeoSpatial, Munich, Germany) was used  to develop the OBIA algorithm, so that ‘objects’ representing each tree were created by joining  adjacent pixels with homogenous spectral values. The procedure consisted of four steps, as follows:  (1) segmentation; (2) classification of trees and bare soil; (3) buffering plant edges; (4) retrieving of  spectral information, and (5) data export for statistical analysis. Ten central pixels were selected and  averaged from each tree to obtain its spectral signature and thus avoiding mixed pixels.

Hyperspectral signatures analysis The spectral information of the peach trees delivered by the OBIA algorithm was grouped by severity  classes (healthy, low, medium and high) and subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) at the 0.01  level of significance by a Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) range test with JMP version  12 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Firstly, spectral variations were explored in the  healthy class as affected by the K-treatment and by the soil management. Then, spectral variations  were researched in the image wavebands as affected by the severity classes.

## Results and discussion

Significant differences in peach canopy hyperspectral profiles were observed in all analysis performed,  i.e. according to the K-treatments; to the soil managements; and to the severity classes, although in  different regions of the spectrum and in different number of image wavebands (Table 1). Therefore,  these results confirm the potential of using hyperspectral camera information to discriminate peach  trees under different agricultural practices and also with different development stages of peach rust.

Spectral differences in the healthy trees by K-treatment Figure 2 shows the hyperspectral signature of healthy peach trees under two K-treatments: 1 K  and 5 K. Despite obtaining potential to discriminate 1K-treated from 5 K-treated peach trees, it  was only feasible in a small number of image wavebands (Table 1) in the red-edge and NIR region.  These results are in agreement with those of de Castro et al. (2015), who identified similar spectral  regions for discriminating laurel wilt, Phytophthora root rot and healthy avocado plants using neural  networks. These spectral regions are related to the amount of pigments in the leaves, so that any  change in biochemical or cell structure is reflected in the spectral value changes of red-edge and NIR.

Spectral differences in the healthy trees by soil management In the case of soil management, many image wavebands in the visible region (Green and Red) allowed  the discrimination of trees whose root collars were excavated from trees with a conventional soil  management (Table 1). These results prove that although it is a soil treatment around the roots,  the peach trees are clearly affected as shown in the spectral profiles (Figure 3). These results should  be evaluated in further analysis in order to relate those spectral changes to the biochemical and  physiological changes in the trees.

Spectral differences by severity classes The results documented sufficient spectral differences among the four stages of peach rust  development, i.e. healthy, low, medium and high, and indicated the spectral regions to examine for  peach rust detection, specifically in green, red and NIR regions (Table 1). Although no significant  differences were obtained for the four classes in the red-edge region, it was possible to separate  healthy, low severity and a third class composed of medium and late classes (Figure 4). Therefore,  these results proved that the hyperspectral signature of peach is modified by peach rust and that it is  possible to determine variations in the spectral signature caused by any stage of disease development  using the combination of hyperspectral camera and UAV platform. Although the analysis performed found spectral differences in various spectral regions, ratios and  vegetation indices derived from these image wavebands should be evaluated in further analysis to  improve the discrimination capacity. In addition, multivariable statistical analysis should be carried  out in order to assess the effect of these agricultural practices on mitigating of peach rust damage. Further analysis should be performed to improve the discrimination capacity and assess the effect  of the agricultural practices on mitigating of peach rust damage. The next step of this research  program will be to develop of a robust OBIA algorithm for the rapid and accurate map of affected  trees that allow designing site-specific management strategy of peach orchards.

## Conclusions

This investigation explored the potential of hyperspectral images collected from an UAV to  evaluate the effect on the spectral signature of two common agricultural practices in peach crops,  i.e. potassium spray and root-collar excavation, as well as of the development stages of a major  disease of this crop, peach rust. Significant differences in peach canopy hyperspectral profiles were  observed in the analysis performed. In addition, the image wavebands that efficiently discriminated  the evaluated factors, i.e. 1 K vs 5 K; excavated vs non-excavated; and healthy vs low vs medium vs  high class, were identified.
